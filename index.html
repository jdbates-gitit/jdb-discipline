Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 25

There was something undefined and complete, coming into existence before Heaven and Earth. How still it was and formless, standing alone, and undergoing no change, reaching everywhere and in no danger (of being exhausted)! It may be regarded as the Mother of all things. I do not know its name, and I give it the designation of the Tao (the Way or Course). Making an effort (further) to give it a name I call it The Great. Great, it passes on (in constant flow). Passing on, it becomes remote. Having become remote, it returns. Therefore the Tao is great; Heaven is great; Earth is great; and the (sage) king is also great. In the universe there are four that are great, and the (sage) king is one of them. Man takes his law from the Earth; the Earth takes its law from Heaven; Heaven takes its law from the Tao. The law of the Tao is its being what it is.

What it's pointing at

Before all names, all forms, all the noise we make about things, there is a vast, undivided aliveness—the Tao—that simply *is*. It doesn't try, doesn't strain, doesn't run out. Everything that exists—you, me, the earth, heaven—flows from and returns to this inexhaustible source. The deepest law isn't something imposed from outside; it's the nature of things to be themselves, endlessly and without effort.

Read against today

In a world of endless argument about who is right and what must be done, we have forgotten the still ground beneath all doing. We mistake noise for power, position-taking for wisdom, the constant churn of opinions for the Way itself. The chapter asks: what if the greatest strength is not our fierce certainty but our willingness to return—again and again—to what simply is, before we named it and fought over it? In our fragmentation, we have lost touch with the Mother of all things, the one thing that asks nothing of us but presence.

To carry today

Today, notice when you are striving, naming, fixing, dividing—and pause. Return, even for a breath, to the ground beneath thought, the stillness before preference. This is not passivity; it is alignment. The Tao does not exhaust itself; neither must you.

Again virtue declined. Yao and Shun ruled the empire. Systems of government and moral reform were introduced. Man's original integrity was scattered. Goodness led him astray from Tao;

But for goodness, evil could not exist.

his actions imperilled his virtue.

As opposed to inaction.

Then he discarded natural instinct and took up with the intellectual. Mind was pitted against mind, but it was impossible thus to settle the empire. So art and learning were added. But art obliterated the original constitution, and learning overwhelmed mind; upon which confusion set in, and man was unable to revert to his natural instincts, to the condition in which he at first existed.

Thus it may be said that the world destroys Tao, and that Tao destroys the world. And the world and Tao thus mutually destroying each other, how can the men of Tao elevate the world, and how can the world elevate Tao? Tao cannot elevate the world; neither can the world elevate Tao. Though the Sages were not to dwell on mountain and in forest, their virtue would still be hidden;—hidden, but not by themselves.

What it's pointing at

Chuang Tzu traces how human civilization—even when well-intentioned—fragments us. Each layer of improvement (agriculture, government, moral teaching, learning) sounds like progress but actually distances us from the seamless responsiveness we had before. The passage suggests that goodness itself, once named and systematized, creates its opposite; that trying to fix the world through effort and intellect is precisely what breaks it. The sages stay hidden not through hiding, but through never having left the original wholeness.

Read against today

We live inside the systems Chuang Tzu describes—we're the inheritors of all those layers of improvement. The sting of this passage is that it doesn't blame ambition or wrongdoing; it identifies something subtler: the moment we name what's good and build structures around it, we've already begun to lose it. For someone in recovery, this lands differently than blame. It points toward something prior to all the effort—not a failure of willpower, but a recognition that the trying itself can obscure the natural return that's always available.

The thread between them

Where the Tao Te Ching calls us to recognize the Mother of all things—that which 'undergoes no change' and reaches everywhere—Chuang Tzu shows us how each well-made system (government, morality, knowledge) gradually obscures that formless ground. Both texts are saying the same thing: return to what requires no effort. The difference is that one states it as law, the other as a cautionary story about what we lose when we forget.
