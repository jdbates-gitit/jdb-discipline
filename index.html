Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 63

(It is the way of the Tao) to act without (thinking of) acting; to conduct affairs without (feeling the) trouble of them; to taste without discerning any flavour; to consider what is small as great, and a few as many; and to recompense injury with kindness. (The master of it) anticipates things that are difficult while they are easy, and does things that would become great while they are small. All difficult things in the world are sure to arise from a previous state in which they were easy, and all great things from one in which they were small. Therefore the sage, while he never does what is great, is able on that account to accomplish the greatest things. He who lightly promises is sure to keep but little faith; he who is continually thinking things easy is sure to find them difficult. Therefore the sage sees difficulty even in what seems easy, and so never has any difficulties.

What it's pointing at

This chapter teaches that mastery comes not from forcing or striving, but from meeting each moment with ease and attention. The sage accomplishes great things by starting small, staying present, and responding to difficulty before it hardens into crisis. By seeing complexity in what appears simple, and by repaying harm with kindness, we avoid the trap of overconfidence that makes small troubles become large ones.

Read against today

We live in an age of grand gestures and swift judgments, where problems are declared solved and enemies are declared irredeemable. The chapter's quiet insistence—that difficulty grows from what we treat as easy, that great harm comes from small neglect—speaks directly to our condition. We promise much and deliver little; we treat the intricate as simple; we meet injury with injury and wonder why the wound deepens. The world's noise tells us to act boldly and think less. This chapter suggests the opposite: that true strength lies in patience, in seeing the small seed of what will become large, in the humble work of tending rather than conquering.

To carry today

Today, notice one thing you're treating as easy or small that might deserve more care. Before you react to anything that frustrates you, pause and ask: what small neglect led here? Where can you respond with kindness instead of matching the tone you've received?

He had a pitcher in his hand, with which he was bringing up water and pouring it into the ditch,—great labour with very little result.

What it's pointing at

The old man at the well works hard, carrying water pitcher by pitcher into his garden ditch—great effort for small result. Chi Ch'ê's teaching suggests there is another way: influence that operates *through* natural inclination rather than against it, so quietly that people don't feel they're being moved. The sage doesn't display his tools or his labor. He works with what wants to flow, not against it.

Read against today

We live in a culture that equates visibility with effectiveness—the more we can see ourselves working, striving, optimizing, the more real the work feels. But this passage asks: what if the most transformative influence is the kind no one notices happening? What if your recovery, your practice, your integrity matter most precisely when you're not performing them for an audience, not even for yourself?

The thread between them

The Tao Te Ching compresses this into paradox—'act without acting,' accomplish the greatest by never doing what is great. The Chuang Tzu shows it unfolding in time: watch the old man exhaust himself with the pitcher while the sage's influence ripples through hearts 'unconsciously of its operation.' Same teaching, different speed—one frozen into a mirror, one moving as a story, but both asking you to drop the pitcher and find the natural gradient.
