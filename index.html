Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 36

When one is about to take an inspiration, he is sure to make a (previous) expiration; when he is going to weaken another, he will first strengthen him; when he is going to overthrow another, he will first have raised him up; when he is going to despoil another, he will first have made gifts to him:--this is called 'Hiding the light (of his procedure).' The soft overcomes the hard; and the weak the strong. Fishes should not be taken from the deep; instruments for the profit of a state should not be shown to the people.

What it's pointing at

This chapter teaches that all real change moves through paradox: you must exhale before inhaling, weaken yourself before strengthening others, give before you take. The Tao works by indirection—by hiding its intention. Softness and yielding are more powerful than force because they move with reality rather than against it. What appears weak is often doing the deepest work.

Read against today

We live in a culture of visible power—shouting our intentions, demanding immediate results, believing strength means showing force. Yet this chapter suggests we have forgotten how change actually moves in the world. We watch leaders and nations attempt to dominate before building trust, grasp before giving, and we see the friction and breakdown that follows. The deeper wisdom here is that lasting influence moves quietly, often unseen. In a time of division and display, the Tao points toward a different kind of strength: the person or nation that can breathe out, that can gift without announcement, that can appear weak while remaining unshakeable.

To carry today

Today, notice one place where you are trying to control or display strength—in a conversation, a decision, a worry. Can you exhale that grip, even slightly? The work that matters often happens in the invisible spaces. Trust the soft thing for just this one day.

Heraclitus · no contact, same mountain

(78) And it is the same thing in us that is quick and dead, awake and asleep, young and old; the former are shifted and 153become the latter, and the latter in turn are shifted and become the former. R. P. 47.

What it's pointing at

Heraclitus is saying that the same stuff in us—the living and the dead, the waking and the sleeping—is constantly trading places. There is no fixed identity, only continuous transformation. What we call 'self' is less like a thing and more like a river where the water is always different but the flow remains. The opposites are not enemies; they are partners in a single, unified becoming.

Read against today

In recovery and in practice, you know this already in your bones: the person who wanted to drink and the person who doesn't are made of the same flesh. The shame of yesterday and the clarity of today are not different people—they are the same person shifting. There is nothing to purge, only to watch the turning. When you feel yourself weakening or strengthening, awake or asleep in the day, you are not failing the practice; you are the practice, watching itself flow.

The thread between them

Today's Tao speaks of hidden reversals—strength given before weakness is imposed, raising before overthrowing—as the method of the soft. Heraclitus arrives at the same recognition from a completely different shore: that opposites don't stay put, that death and life are continuous partners in the same body, that transformation itself is the only constant. Neither tradition had access to the other, yet both saw that the deepest power lies not in locking things in place but in understanding they are already moving through each other.
