Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 73

He whose boldness appears in his daring (to do wrong, in defiance of the laws) is put to death; he whose boldness appears in his not daring (to do so) lives on. Of these two cases the one appears to be advantageous, and the other to be injurious. But

When Heaven's anger smites a man, Who the cause shall truly scan?

On this account the sage feels a difficulty (as to what to do in the former case). It is the way of Heaven not to strive, and yet it skilfully overcomes; not to speak, and yet it is skilful in obtaining a reply; does not call, and yet men come to it of themselves. Its demonstrations are quiet, and yet its plans are skilful and effective. The meshes of the net of Heaven are large; far apart, but letting nothing escape.

What it's pointing at

The chapter teaches that true strength lies not in aggressive force, but in restraint and alignment with natural law. The sage recognizes that the universe operates through quiet effectiveness—it doesn't demand or coerce, yet everything moves toward it. When we act from desperate grasping or defiant will, we create suffering; when we act from understanding and non-resistance, we find ourselves held by something larger than ourselves.

Read against today

We live in a time of visible striving—endless argument, positioning, the noise of people demanding to be heard and seen. The chapter whispers that this very boldness often defeats itself, while the quiet work of truth, compassion, and listening goes on beneath the surface, largely unnoticed. We mistake silence for weakness and volume for power, yet Heaven's net catches what matters most without fanfare. The world's current fever—its hunger to control outcomes through force of will and speech—is precisely what this ancient teaching warns against. What if the deepest change in ourselves and our culture comes not from our boldness, but from our willingness to not-do, to listen, to let ourselves be changed?

To carry today

Today, notice where you are striving and where you are flowing. When you feel the urge to convince, to prove, to force—pause and ask if there is a quieter way forward. The universe is patient and large-meshed; nothing truly escapes it. You can rest your tired arms.

Chuang Tzu · same root, different voice
The Great Supreme (excerpt)

"Got rid of everything!" said Confucius eagerly. "What do you mean by that?"

"I have freed myself from my body," answered Yen Hui. "I have discarded my reasoning powers. And by thus getting rid of body and mind, I have become One with the Infinite. This is what I mean by getting rid of everything."

"If you have become One," cried Confucius, "there can be no room for bias. If you have passed into space, you are indeed without beginning or end. And if you have really attained to this, I trust to be allowed to follow in your steps."

Tzŭ Yü and Tzŭ Sang were friends. Once when it had rained for ten days, Tzŭ Yü said, "Tzŭ Sang is dangerously ill." So he packed up some food and went to see him.

In accordance with the exigencies of mortality. How Tzŭ Yü knew that his friend was ill is not clear. An attempt has been made by one commentator on the basis of animal magnetism, in which the Chinese have believed for centuries.

Arriving at the door, he heard something between singing and lamentation, accompanied with the sound of music, as follows:—

"O father! O mother! O Heaven! O Man!"

These words seemed to be uttered with a great effort; whereupon Tzŭ Yü went in and asked what it all meant.

What it's pointing at

Yen Hui describes liberation as a radical surrender—not acquiring wisdom, but releasing the body's demands and the mind's constant sorting and judging. He steps out of the machinery of self entirely and becomes transparent to what moves through all things. His friend Tzŭ Sang, hearing the rain fall for ten days, sings and laments without separation between joy and sorrow, father and heaven—also moving through life without the rigid boundaries the ordinary mind maintains.

Read against today

In recovery and contemplative practice, we meet this teaching when we stop fighting our circumstances and our own nature. The voice that sings and laments at once—neither resisting difficulty nor collapsing into it—is the voice of someone who has released the exhausting project of managing outcomes. This is how the day opens when we stop insisting life should feel a particular way.

The thread between them

Today's Tao teaches that Heaven's net 'lets nothing escape' through non-action and quiet demonstration; the Chuang Tzu shows this same capture happening through Yen Hui's complete surrender and Tzŭ Sang's seamless song—different doors into the same truth that when we stop striving to control what comes through the meshes, we discover we were never separate from it.
