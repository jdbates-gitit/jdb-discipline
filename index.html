Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 18

When the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed, benevolence and righteousness came into vogue. (Then) appeared wisdom and shrewdness, and there ensued great hypocrisy. When harmony no longer prevailed throughout the six kinships, filial sons found their manifestation; when the states and clans fell into disorder, loyal ministers appeared.

What it's pointing at

When we lose touch with the natural way—when we abandon presence and go unconscious—we compensate by inventing systems of morality, cleverness, and performance. Benevolence becomes a strategy rather than a spontaneous act. The chapter suggests that elaborate virtue is often a symptom of its absence: we advertise what we've lost. In wholeness, these things simply arise without fanfare.

Read against today

We live in an age of declared values and performed integrity. Our institutions trumpet benevolence while systems remain fractured. We see wisdom and expertise everywhere—endless commentary, analysis, optimization—yet the underlying coherence continues to fray. The chapter holds up a mirror: perhaps all this urgent righteousness, this flood of competing moral certainties, is itself a sign that we've drifted from something simpler and truer. The noise of our principles may be the echo of their absence.

To carry today

Today, notice where you're trying hard to be good, and where goodness simply flows. Notice the difference between a performed kindness and an unsummoned one. When you feel pulled to prove your worth or correctness, pause—this may be the chapter speaking, pointing you back to what needs no announcement.

What it's pointing at

Chuang Tzu teaches that our suffering comes from dividing the world into opposites—true and false, right and wrong, self and other—and then defending one side. The cure is not to choose better, but to stop choosing: to rest in a state where the mind becomes like still water, where all distinctions dissolve into a single flowing responsiveness. This dissolution isn't emptiness; it's the deepest clarity, what he calls 'light out of darkness.'

Read against today

In recovery, we learn that the rigid categories we once clung to—'I am bad,' 'they are the problem,' 'this craving is me'—lose their grip when we stop arguing for them. The obsessive clarity of the addicted mind, always dividing the world into what we want and what we must escape, exhausts itself. When we finally stop defending a position and simply rest in the flow of what is—breath, sensation, presence—we find we can move more skillfully than our best strategies ever allowed.

The thread between them

The Tao Te Ching says that when people abandoned the Great Way, they invented benevolence and righteousness—splitting wholeness into codes and categories. Chuang Tzu shows us why: the moment we take a position (even a moral one), we've already left the center where 'all distinctions are merged in One.' Both texts are saying the same thing—that our making of distinctions is itself the wound—but the Tao speaks it as law while Chuang Tzu teaches it through the story of Tzŭ Ch'i 'burying himself,' letting the reader feel what non-division actually tastes like.
