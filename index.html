Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 12

Colour's five hues from th' eyes their sight will take; Music's five notes the ears as deaf can make; The flavours five deprive the mouth of taste; The chariot course, and the wild hunting waste Make mad the mind; and objects rare and strange, Sought for, men's conduct will to evil change. Therefore the sage seeks to satisfy (the craving of) the belly, and not the (insatiable longing of the) eyes. He puts from him the latter, and prefers to seek the former.

What it's pointing at

The five senses, when overstimulated by excess, become numb rather than alive. We mistake craving for living—chasing novelty, status, and sensation until we've exhausted ourselves and lost the ability to enjoy simple things. The sage chooses differently: attending to basic needs and inner quiet rather than endless wanting. This is not deprivation; it's freedom.

Read against today

We live in a civilization engineered to amplify craving. Every screen flashes with outrage, desire, and comparison. The constant noise of competing claims, curated images, and manufactured urgency leaves us depleted—our senses overwhelmed into numbness, our attention fractured, our peace inaccessible. The chapter whispers something radical: that our exhaustion is not a sign of living fully, but of being caught in a trap. In a world that profits from our hunger, choosing simplicity and contentment becomes a quiet act of resistance and sanity.

To carry today

Today, notice what you're reaching for before you reach for it. Is it feeding something real in you, or feeding a hunger that grows larger with each bite? This morning, let one thing be enough—one cup of coffee, one conversation, one task done well. See what becomes possible when you stop searching for more.

Epictetus · freedom at the boundary of choice
Encheiridion XXV (excerpt)

Has any man been preferred before you at a banquet, or in being saluted, or in being invited to a consultation? If these things are good, you ought to rejoice that he has obtained them; but if bad, be not grieved because you have not obtained them. And remember that you cannot, if you do not the same things in order to obtain what is not in our own power, be considered worthy of the same (equal) things. For how can a man obtain an equal share with another when he does not visit a man’s doors as that other man does; when he does not attend him when he goes abroad, as the other man does; when he does not praise (flatter) him as another does? You will be unjust then and insatiable, if you do not part with the price, in return for which those things are sold, and if you wish to obtain them for nothing. Well, what is the price of lettuces? An obolus perhaps. If then a man gives up the obolus, and receives the lettuces, and if you do not give up the obolus and do not obtain the lettuces, do not suppose that you receive less than he who has got the lettuces; for as he has the lettuces, so...

What it's pointing at

Epictetus argues that external goods—status, honor, invitations—have a real price: you must pay it in currency of your own choices and dignity. If you will not flatter, attend, visit, or scheme, then you cannot fairly claim to deserve what those actions purchase. The question is not whether these things are good or bad in themselves, but whether you are willing to become the person who obtains them, and at what cost to your freedom of judgment.

Read against today

We live in an age of relentless comparison and invisible pricing. The cost of social validation has been obscured by algorithms and metrics that feel automatic, yet the price remains: your attention, your public performance, your careful curation of desire. Epictetus forces us to look directly at the transaction. When you resent not receiving what you have not actually paid for—in honest attention, in presence, in the full price the other person paid—you are asking to have it both ways. That is what he calls unjust and insatiable.

The Echo · Chuang Tzu
The Tao of God (excerpt)

"No," answered the Duke; "they are dead."

"Then the words your Highness is studying," rejoined the wheelwright, "are only the dregs of the ancients."

"What do you mean, sirrah!" cried the Duke, "by interfering with what I read? Explain yourself, or you shall die."

"Let me take an illustration," said the wheelwright, "from my own trade. In making a wheel, if you work too slowly, you can't make it firm; if you work too fast, the spokes won't fit in. You must go neither too slowly nor too fast. There must be...

The wheelwright's instruction—'neither too slowly nor too fast'—echoes in Epictetus's relentless logic, but from the opposite direction. Chuang Tzu speaks of a rhythm and feel that cannot be hurried or delayed without losing the work itself. Epictetus speaks of clear-eyed choice and the cost you must consciously bear. Together they ask: are you trying to obtain something by refusing to do what obtaining it requires, or are you caught in a false pace that prevents you from seeing what you actually want?

The Confluence
Three voices, one morning — without making them say the same thing.

Where they meet

All three reject the tyranny of the five hues, the five notes—the insatiable grasping at objects and honors that clouds judgment. Epictetus and Chuang Tzu both insist on seeing clearly what is actually happening in the transaction or the work: not through emotion or resentment, but through direct observation. The Tao Te Ching teaches the belly's satisfaction; Epictetus and Chuang Tzu together teach the satisfaction of seeing the price you are and are not willing to pay.

Where they part

The Tao Te Ching counsels withdrawal from the five senses themselves—not to engage in the competition at all. Epictetus does not ask you to withdraw; he asks you to be honest about what you choose and what it costs. Chuang Tzu speaks of a natural rhythm that emerges when forcing ceases. Epictetus speaks of rigorous individual accountability. The Sage avoids the hunt; Epictetus enters it with open eyes, holding himself responsible for every judgment he makes along the way.

To carry today

Notice one moment today when you feel resentment about something you have not received. Before judging it as unfair, ask silently: What exact price was paid by the person who received it? Am I unwilling to pay that price, or unwilling to admit I am paying it? Let the answer clarify your next choice, without guilt or performance.
