Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 59

For regulating the human (in our constitution) and rendering the (proper) service to the heavenly, there is nothing like moderation. It is only by this moderation that there is effected an early return (to man's normal state). That early return is what I call the repeated accumulation of the attributes (of the Tao). With that repeated accumulation of those attributes, there comes the subjugation (of every obstacle to such return). Of this subjugation we know not what shall be the limit; and when one knows not what the limit shall be, he may be the ruler of a state. He who possesses the mother of the state may continue long. His case is like that (of the plant) of which we say that its roots are deep and its flower stalks firm:--this is the way to secure that its enduring life shall long be seen.

What it's pointing at

This chapter teaches that moderation—restraint, balance, living within proper limits—is the foundation of both inner wholeness and stable outer life. By practicing this discipline repeatedly, we naturally return to our truest nature and dissolve obstacles. When we stop grasping and demanding to know where the boundary is, we become capable of genuine leadership. Deep roots and firm stalks come not from constant growth, but from steady, moderate nourishment.

Read against today

We live in a culture that treats moderation as failure and limits as insults. The noise never stops, the outrage refreshes by the hour, and everyone seems convinced that more—more certainty, more volume, more victory—will finally satisfy. Yet the chapter whispers something the frenzy obscures: that not-knowing the limit, that willingness to let go of absolute answers, is where real strength lives. In a world of rigid positions and infinite appetite, the person who practices restraint becomes almost invisibly powerful, like a deep-rooted plant that simply endures while the shallow-rooted ones snap.

To carry today

Today, notice one moment when you reach for more—more information, more certainty, more assurance that you're right—and gently pause instead. In that small pause, you're tending your roots. Return to what is enough, what is true without fanfare, and watch how a quiet strength begins to settle in you.

What it's pointing at

The sword of the Son of Heaven is not a weapon at all—it is the natural order itself, the integrated whole of geography, season, and elemental force operating without strain or intention. It does not advance or retreat, does not exert force, yet nothing resists it. Chuang Tzu is showing that true power lies in alignment with what is, not in the wielding of will.

Read against today

In recovery, we learn that our attempts to force change—to make the sword advance through sheer effort—exhaust us and often backfire. The deeper shift comes through showing up, staying still with what is difficult, letting the seasons of healing work. When we stop trying to control the outcome and instead tend to the roots, the transformation happens on its own timeline, in its own way.

The thread between them

Where the Tao Te Ching teaches moderation and 'early return' through compressed instruction, Chuang Tzu tells the same teaching as a vision of immovable power: both are pointing at the paradox that the greatest strength comes from ceasing to strain. The 'repeated accumulation of attributes' in the Tao becomes, in the sword passage, the natural accumulation of all elements in their proper place—neither text argues for effort, both for rootedness.
