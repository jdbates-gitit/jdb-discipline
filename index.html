Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 75

The people suffer from famine because of the multitude of taxes consumed by their superiors. It is through this that they suffer famine. The people are difficult to govern because of the (excessive) agency of their superiors (in governing them). It is through this that they are difficult to govern. The people make light of dying because of the greatness of their labours in seeking for the means of living. It is this which makes them think light of dying. Thus it is that to leave the subject of living altogether out of view is better than to set a high value on it.

What it's pointing at

When those in power extract too much—through taxation, control, or the burden of impossible standards—people suffer and become ungovernable. The chapter suggests that when survival itself becomes a grinding struggle, people lose their grip on life. The teaching points to a paradox: by ceasing to grasp so tightly at life, by stepping back from the feverish pursuit of security and control, we find what we actually need.

Read against today

We live in a culture of relentless consumption and control—of resources, attention, information, each other's behavior. Those with power often believe more intervention, more management, more extraction will solve problems, yet these only deepen the suffering they mean to address. We see the fruits everywhere: exhaustion, despair, a kind of spiritual suffocation. The Tao Te Ching, written millennia ago, recognizes this pattern with clarity. It invites us to notice that our frantic grasping—for safety, status, certainty—may be the very thing that undoes us, and that wisdom sometimes means laying the burden down.

To carry today

Today, notice where you are grasping—at control, at an outcome, at your own survival or image. Can you practice, just for an hour, letting that grip soften? Not recklessness, but trust. This is how you remember you are alive.

What it's pointing at

The Duke learns that true governance isn't about doing more—managing, controlling, deciding—but about creating conditions where people naturally find balance, like water leveling a surface without effort or overflow. Virtue that doesn't announce itself, that stays quiet within and simply radiates outward, is more regenerative than all the active intervention a ruler can muster. The perfect person doesn't enter the soul's domain or meddle with the organism; they swim with the tide.

Read against today

We live in a time of constant intervention—in our own lives, in others' lives, in systems we think we can fix by trying harder. The exhaustion many feel comes not from insufficient effort but from the depletion that comes when we refuse to stop trying. This passage suggests that the deepest change happens when we stop being so visibly, anxiously responsible and instead embody a kind of settled integrity that influences others simply by its presence. It's radical: your peace becomes contagious without you selling it.

The thread between them

Chapter 75 names the problem (excessive agency, multitude of taxes, the grinding effort of those governed) while Chuang Tzu shows the solution in action: the Duke's shift from 'caring for people's lives' through striving to understanding that water's quiet repose does the leveling. Both texts point to the same mechanism—that our over-doing unmakes things, and our stepping back allows natural rightness to emerge—but the Tao speaks it as diagnosis and antidote, while the story lets it unfold through the Duke's own recognition.
