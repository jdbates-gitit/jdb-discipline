01 · The question

When I explain my recovery or my faith to others, am I pointing toward living differently, or performing knowledge I haven't yet embodied?

02 · Today's lead · Epictetus

Encheiridion XLIX

George Long translation · public domain

When a man is proud because he can understand and explain the writings of Chrysippus, say to yourself, If Chrysippus had not written obscurely, this man would have had nothing to be proud of. But what is it that I wish? To understand nature and to follow it. I inquire therefore who is the interpreter? and when I have heard that it is Chrysippus, I come to him (the interpreter). But I do not understand what is written, and therefore I seek the interpreter. And so far there is yet nothing to be proud of. But when I shall have found the interpreter, the thing that remains is to use the precepts (the lessons). This itself is the only thing to be proud of. But if I shall admire the exposition, what else have I been made unless a grammarian instead of a philosopher? except in one thing, that I am explaining Chrysippus instead of Homer. When, then, any man says to me, Read Chrysippus to me, I rather blush, when I cannot show my acts like to and consistent with his words.

A lens for today

Epictetus cuts through a common trap: mistaking understanding for transformation. We can read widely, interpret deeply, sound wise—and still live small. The real work is not mastering the text but letting the text master us through consistent, visible action. In recovery, this means the difference between explaining the program and living it.

03 · The companion · Heraclitus

Fragment 108

Fragments · John Burnet translation · public domain

(108, 109) It is best to hide folly; but it is hard in times of relaxation, over our cups.

What this voice adds

Heraclitus arrives with a sober warning: relaxation and fellowship dissolve our guard. We hide our foolishness best when we're alert, but wine and ease undo that vigilance. He doesn't soften the tension—he holds it. Wisdom isn't a permanent achievement; it's a daily, difficult practice against our own nature.

04 · The Echo · Brother Lawrence

Eighth Letter · excerpt

The Practice of the Presence of God · 1895 edition · public domain

One way to re-collect the mind easily in the time of prayer, and preserve it more in tranquility, is not to let it wander too far at other times: you should keep it strictly in the presence of GOD; and being accustomed to think of Him often, you will find it easy to keep your mind calm in the time of prayer, or at least to recall it from its wanderings.

I have told you already at large, in my former letters, of the advantages we may draw from this practice of the presence of GOD: let us set about it seriously,…

Brother Lawrence teaches that a steady, simple practice of presence throughout the day—not heroic effort in crisis—keeps the mind available to God. This echoes both Epictetus's demand for daily living and Heraclitus's warning: steady attention is harder than impressive knowledge.

05 · The Confluence

Where they meet.
Where they part.

All three agree that knowledge, faith, and virtue are not ornaments but practices embedded in ordinary life. Epictetus demands visible acts; Heraclitus warns that even the aware person stumbles in unguarded moments; Brother Lawrence offers the antidote—not perfection, but the habit of returning to God's presence. Where they differ: Epictetus emphasizes will and choice; Heraclitus emphasizes the hidden struggle and flux; Brother Lawrence emphasizes grace and simple, continuous communion. Together they forbid both self-righteousness and despair.

Take this into the day

In the next conversation about my faith or recovery, can I listen more than explain, and let my life answer instead?
