Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 39

The things which from of old have got the One (the Tao) are--

Heaven which by it is bright and pure; Earth rendered thereby firm and sure; Spirits with powers by it supplied; Valleys kept full throughout their void All creatures which through it do live Princes and kings who from it get The model which to all they give.

All these are the results of the One (Tao). If heaven were not thus pure, it soon would rend; If earth were not thus sure, 'twould break and bend; Without these powers, the spirits soon would fail; If not so filled, the drought would parch each vale; Without that life, creatures would pass away; Princes and kings, without that moral sway, However grand and high, would all decay. Thus it is that dignity finds its (firm) root in its (previous) meanness, and what is lofty finds its stability in the lowness (from which it rises). Hence princes and kings call themselves 'Orphans,' 'Men of small virtue,' and as 'Carriages without a nave.' Is not this an acknowledgment that in their considering themselves mean they see the foundation of their dignity? So it is that in the enumeration of the different parts of a carriage we do not come on what makes it answer the ends of a carriage. They do not wish to show themselves elegant-looking as jade, but (prefer) to be coarse-looking as an (ordinary) stone.

What it's pointing at

The Tao sustains all things—heaven, earth, spirits, valleys, creatures, and leaders—by holding them in balance through emptiness and humility. True power and stability come not from grasping or display, but from understanding your own smallness and remaining willing to serve. A wheel works not because of the rim or spokes, but because of the hollow hub; a cup holds water because of the space within. The greatest leaders know this secret: they stay low so nothing can topple them.

Read against today

We live in a time of relentless self-promotion and the hunger for visibility, where emptiness is mistaken for weakness and humility for failure. Nations, movements, and individuals compete to appear strong, certain, and full—never acknowledging the void within them or the ground beneath their feet. Yet this chapter asks: what happens when we lose the capacity to be small, to listen, to hold space rather than fill it? The world's fracturing may come not from our disagreements alone, but from our collective refusal to find dignity in the low place, to admit what we do not know, to serve something larger than ourselves.

To carry today

Today, notice where you are trying to appear full, certain, or grand—in a conversation, a decision, or simply in how you move through the world. See if you can find one moment to choose the hollow place instead: to listen more than speak, to ask rather than declare, to let the space do the work. This is not weakness. This is where real strength lives.

Heraclitus · no contact, same mountain

(108, 109) It is best to hide folly; but it is hard in times of relaxation, over our cups.

What it's pointing at

Heraclitus warns that concealing foolishness becomes nearly impossible once we relax our guard—especially in moments of ease and communion with others. The insight is not moral scolding but recognition: our pretense requires constant vigilance, and that vigilance itself is a form of strain we cannot indefinitely maintain. In relaxation, what we truly are emerges.

Read against today

There is a particular exhaustion in the modern performance of competence and polish. We are constantly curating, explaining, justifying our standing. The relief that comes in genuine rest—with trusted people, or alone—is partly the relief of dropping the carriage's ornament. In those unguarded moments, we meet ourselves as we are, which is often simpler and less shameful than we feared.

The thread between them

Both texts point to the same reversal: that true strength lies in surrendering the posture of strength. The Tao teaches that princes call themselves orphans and prefer coarse stone to jade—the deliberate embrace of plainness as foundation. Heraclitus arrives at the same door from the opposite direction: we cannot hide our ordinariness, and the attempt to do so is what exhausts us most. One text prescribes the emptying; the other describes what happens when we stop trying to fill ourselves.
