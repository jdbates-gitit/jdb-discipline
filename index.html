Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 32

The Tao, considered as unchanging, has no name. Though in its primordial simplicity it may be small, the whole world dares not deal with (one embodying) it as a minister. If a feudal prince or the king could guard and hold it, all would spontaneously submit themselves to him. Heaven and Earth (under its guidance) unite together and send down the sweet dew, which, without the directions of men, reaches equally everywhere as of its own accord. As soon as it proceeds to action, it has a name. When it once has that name, (men) can know to rest in it. When they know to rest in it, they can be free from all risk of failure and error. The relation of the Tao to all the world is like that of the great rivers and seas to the streams from the valleys.

What it's pointing at

The Tao in its truest nature is nameless and simple—it doesn't need labels or complicated philosophy. But the moment we try to act on it or describe it, it takes form and becomes knowable. The teaching is that when we stop grasping and simply rest in this knowing—in the quiet ground of things—we naturally align with how the world works, like water flowing downhill without effort or instruction.

Read against today

We live in an age of endless naming, labeling, and competing claims about truth. We mistake constant action and definition for power, drowning in commentary while the simple Tao operates silently beneath all our noise. The world's suffering often comes not from the Tao itself but from our refusal to rest in it—our need to grasp, control, make everything mean something in our story. When we look at division and conflict, we see people who have forgotten that there is something prior to all our categories, something that reaches equally to all. This chapter whispers: the peace you're seeking doesn't live in your next argument or policy—it lives in the rest you stop resisting.

To carry today

Today, notice where you are naming and defining instead of resting. When tension arises—whether inside you or around you—pause and remember the image of dew falling equally everywhere, needing no one's permission or direction. Can you trust, for just this morning, that some things work better when you let them be?

Chuang Tzu · same root, different voice
On Declining Power (excerpt)

of Chêng, saying, "Lieh Tzŭ is a scholar who has attained to Tao. He lives in your Excellency's State, and yet he is poor. Can it be said that your Excellency does not love scholars?"

Thereupon Tzŭ Yang gave orders that Lieh Tzŭ should be supplied with food. But when Lieh Tzŭ saw the messengers, he bowed twice and declined.

When the messengers had gone, and Lieh Tzŭ went within, his wife gazed at him, and beating her breast said, "I have heard that the wife and children of a man of Tao are happy and joyful. But see how hungry I am. His Excellency sent you food, and you would not take it. Is not this flying in the face of Providence?"

"His Excellency did not know me personally," answered Lieh Tzŭ with a smile. "It was because of what others said about me that he sent me the food. If then men were to speak ill of me, he would also act upon it. For that reason I refused the food."

Subsequently, there was trouble among the people of Chêng, and Tzŭ Yang was slain.

When Prince Chao of the Ch'u State lost his kingdom, he was followed into exile by his butcher, named Yüeh.

On his restoration, as he was distributing rewards to those who had remained faithful to him, he came to the name of Yüeh.

What it's pointing at

Lieh Tzŭ refuses the prince's gift not from pride but from clarity: he knows that reputation—what others say—is a fragile, reversible thing, and to accept based on it would bind him to the prince's changing opinion. His wife sees only hunger; he sees the trap of dependency on names and words. By declining, he keeps himself free from the currents of favor and disfavor that move through the world of men.

Read against today

We live in a time of constant naming—of ourselves, our worth, our standing in the eyes of others. The offer comes sweetly (food, recognition, inclusion), but each acceptance tightens the thread between who we are and what is said about us. The harder we work to secure a good name, the more we become creatures of that name, vulnerable to its reversal. There is a freedom in being unknown, or willing to become unknown again.

The thread between them

Today's Tao chapter teaches that 'as soon as it proceeds to action, it has a name'—the moment the Tao moves into the world, it becomes subject to words and judgment. Lieh Tzŭ's refusal enacts exactly this: he stays in the 'primordial simplicity' without a name, while the prince is enslaved to the names others give him, ultimately to his destruction. The Tao says this in one compressed paradox; the Chuang Tzu shows it unfolding as a man's life, a wife's hunger, and a kingdom's fall.
