Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 61

What makes a great state is its being (like) a low-lying, down-flowing (stream);--it becomes the centre to which tend (all the small states) under heaven. (To illustrate from) the case of all females:--the female always overcomes the male by her stillness. Stillness may be considered (a sort of) abasement. Thus it is that a great state, by condescending to small states, gains them for itself; and that small states, by abasing themselves to a great state, win it over to them. In the one case the abasement leads to gaining adherents, in the other case to procuring favour. The great state only wishes to unite men together and nourish them; a small state only wishes to be received by, and to serve, the other. Each gets what it desires, but the great state must learn to abase itself.

What it's pointing at

True power lies not in dominance but in receptivity and humility. Like water that flows downward and gathers strength through yielding rather than force, greatness emerges when we release the need to control and instead make space for others. Both large and small find what they seek—unity, nourishment, favor—only when they embrace a posture of service rather than superiority.

Read against today

We live in an age of assertion: every voice amplified, every position defended, every group convinced it must prevail to survive. The chapter's mirror shows us a culture of hardness meeting hardness, of constant upward striving where everyone competes for higher ground. Yet the world's actual crises—loneliness, fragmentation, exhaustion—suggest we are parched not from too much yielding but from too little. We have forgotten that the deepest influence moves quietly, that true security comes not from winning arguments but from creating conditions where others feel held and nourished. The noise we mistake for strength may be only the sound of things breaking.

To carry today

Today, notice where you are defending rather than listening, where you are trying to be larger instead of deeper. Can you find one moment to make yourself small—to ask rather than tell, to listen past the first layer, to let someone else's need matter more than your need to be right? This is not weakness; this is where real strength lives.

Epictetus · freedom at the boundary of choice
Encheiridion XXVII

As a mark is not set up for the purpose of missing the aim, so neither does the nature of evil exist in the world.

What it's pointing at

Epictetus cuts through illusion: evil is not a natural part of the world's design, like a target that exists to be missed. This means that suffering, failure, and harm are not built into existence as purposes or inevitabilities. What remains in our actual power is the quality of our judgment—how we interpret what happens and what we choose to desire in response. Freedom lies not in controlling outcomes, but in disciplining our assent to them.

Read against today

We live in a culture that treats failure, rejection, and loss as personal indictments—as if the universe aimed them at us. Epictetus refuses that reading. When you are passed over, dismissed, or hurt, the event is morally neutral; your judgment about what it means, and your choice about how to respond, is where character lives. This is both more demanding and more liberating than blaming circumstance or fate.

The Echo · Chuang Tzu
On Declining Power (excerpt)

A visitor one day mentioned this to Tzŭ Yang

Prime Minister.

of Chêng, saying, "Lieh Tzŭ is a scholar who has attained to Tao. He lives in your Excellency's State, and yet he is poor. Can it be said that your Excellency does not love scholars?"

Thereupon Tzŭ Yang gave orders that Lieh Tzŭ should be supplied with food. But when Lieh Tzŭ saw the messengers, he bowed twice and declined.

When the messengers had gone, and Lieh Tzŭ went within, his wife gazed at him, and beating her breast said, "I have heard that...

Lieh Tzŭ's refusal of the Prime Minister's gift—food offered out of pity for poverty—shows a man who knows the difference between what happens (the offer) and what he chooses to do with his judgment about it (decline with grace). Like Epictetus, Chuang Tzu locates freedom not in circumstance but in the clarity and independence of the response.

The Confluence
Three voices, one morning — without making them say the same thing.

Where they meet

All three texts locate true power in a kind of inner abasement or humility that paradoxically gains what it does not grasp for. The Tao Te Ching says the great state must learn to abase itself; Epictetus says freedom comes from rigorous judgment, not from grasping; Chuang Tzu shows a man poor yet undisturbed, because he has not enslaved his will to his circumstance. Each teaches that real strength is not the force to control outcomes, but the clarity to respond rightly to what is.

Where they part

Epictetus insists on active moral discernment and choice—you must *judge* and *decide* how to respond, and this is your responsibility. The Taoist passages suggest flowing with what is natural, without the muscular effort of constant judgment. Epictetus is stern about duty; Chuang Tzu is serene about non-action. Yet neither counsels passivity or indifference—the difference is in the quality of the effort, not its absence.

To carry today

Today, notice one moment when you are tempted to treat what happened as a verdict on your worth. Pause there. Ask: What is the fact? What is my judgment about it? What do I genuinely choose to desire now? Let Epictetus's rigor meet the Tao's patience in that space.
