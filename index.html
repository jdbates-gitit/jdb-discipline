01 · The question · Humility

When someone contradicts me today, can I notice whether I'm defending a grip on being right, or actually curious about what I'm missing?

02 · Today's lead · Dhammapada

Miscellaneous · verses 290–292

Acharya Buddharakkhita · Buddhist Publication Society, 1985 · used under conditional free-redistribution license

If by renouncing a lesser happiness one may realize a greater happiness, let the wise man renounce the lesser, having regard for the greater.

Entangled by the bonds of hate, he who seeks his own happiness by inflicting pain on others, is never delivered from hatred.

The cankers only increase for those who are arrogant and heedless, who leave undone what should be done and do what should not be done.

A lens for today

The Dhammapada invites a sober arithmetic: some satisfactions cost more than they're worth, especially when we buy them by inflicting pain on others or hardening into arrogance. The teaching is not about crushing desire, but about recognizing which happiness actually liberates and which only binds us tighter to the very thing that hurts. In recovery and spiritual work, this shows up as the difference between a momentary win and a deepening freedom.

03 · The companion · Montaigne

Of The Art Of Conference · excerpt

Essays · Charles Cotton translation, W. C. Hazlitt edition, 1877 · public domain in the USA
Book III · paragraph 7 · sentence 1–4 · Read in context ↗

When any one contradicts me, he raises my attention, not my anger: I advance towards him who controverts, who instructs me; the cause of truth ought to be the common cause both of the one and the other. What will the angry man answer? Passion has already confounded his judgment; agitation has usurped the place of reason. It were not amiss that the decision of our disputes should pass by wager: that there might be a material mark of our losses, to the end we might the better remember them; and that my man might tell me: “Your ignorance and obstinacy cost you last year, at several times, a hundred crowns.” I hail and caress truth in what quarter soever I find it, and cheerfully surrender myself, and open my conquered arms as far off as I can discover it; and, provided it be not too imperiously, take a pleasure in being reproved, and accommodate myself to my accusers, very often more by reason of civility than amendment, loving to gratify and nourish the liberty of admonition by my facility of submitting to it, and this even at my own expense.

What this voice adds

Montaigne does something Dhammapada doesn't quite say: he shows what it actually feels like to meet contradiction without collapse. His curiosity is not grim or dutiful; he takes pleasure in being corrected. He doesn't deny having lost arguments or having ego—he just doesn't let those facts lock him into a posture. He chose to build a habit of openness, and he enjoys it.

04 · The Echo · Epictetus

Encheiridion XIII

George Long translation · public domain

If you would improve, submit to be considered without sense and foolish with respect to externals. Wish to be considered to know nothing; and if you shall seem to some to be a person of importance, distrust yourself. For you should know that it is not easy both to keep your will in a condition conformable to nature and (to secure) external things: but if a man is careful about the one, it is an absolute necessity that he will neglect the other.

Epictetus names the bind: you cannot grip both your own integrity and external validation simultaneously. His medicine is to notice the cost and choose freely. This cuts against Montaigne's ease—Epictetus insists on the sacrifice—yet both refuse to let anger or shame make the choice for us.

05 · The Confluence

Where they meet.
Where they part.

Montaigne shows humility as an actual pleasure once you stop performing certainty. Epictetus warns that the pleasure costs something; you must actively release your claim to look wise. The Dhammapada stands apart: it measures happiness by whether it increases or decreases the bonds of hatred. All three reject the fiction that being corrected is a wound you must absorb. They simply disagree on the texture: Montaigne finds joy in it; Epictetus finds freedom through deliberate surrender; the Dhammapada asks whether your peace actually deepens. No synthesis needed. Each offers a different way to encounter the same choice today.

Attention & presence

Seeing what is here before rushing to explain or change it.

  • Attention — The quality of noticing, listening, and meeting the ordinary.
  • Presence — Participation in this moment rather than living only in anticipation.
  • Stillness — A receptive pause, not withdrawal from necessary action.
  • Clear seeing — Distinguishing what is present from the story added to it.

Freedom & choice

Judgment, responsibility, boundaries, and the next chosen response.

  • Judgment — Examining impressions and opinions before consenting to them.
  • Chosen response — The space between an encounter and the conduct chosen within it.
  • Boundaries — Care for self and others without confusing love with compliance.
  • Courage — Acting responsibly with uncertainty or fear still present.

Change & letting go

Non-forcing, surrender, impermanence, and mortality without resignation.

  • Non-forcing — Skillful participation without coercing a person or a process.
  • Surrender — Releasing ownership of outcomes while remaining responsible; one lens among many.
  • Impermanence — Changing conditions, relationships, selves, and opportunities.
  • Mortality — Finite time and loss as invitations to care, not demands for frantic achievement.

Love & conduct

How kindness, compassion, generosity, and truth become actions.

  • Kindness — Small, concrete forms of considerate conduct.
  • Compassion — Recognizing suffering without making it an identity or a personal failure.
  • Generosity — Giving time, attention, resources, or credit without buying an outcome.
  • Truthfulness — Honest words and conduct without weaponizing honesty.

Belonging & relationship

Friendship, equality, service, and shared life without erasing difference.

  • Belonging — A place in shared life that is not earned by flawless performance.
  • Equal dignity — Seeing people beyond roles, status, usefulness, or resemblance to oneself.
  • Service — Useful contribution without control, martyrdom, or self-erasure.
  • Friendship — Company, listening, mutual care, and allowing oneself to receive.

Wonder & participation

Nature, embodiment, ordinary beauty, joy, and entering the world.

  • Wonder — Encountering life as more than a problem or a resource.
  • Nature — Encounter with the living world on its own terms, not merely as a metaphor.
  • Embodied life — The body, senses, limits, and lived experience without shame or forced celebration.
  • Joy — Pleasure, play, and appreciation that need not be postponed until life is solved.

Growth & practice

Beginning again, patience, humility, and sustained effort without self-punishment.

  • Beginning again — Renewal after error, discouragement, or a lost habit.
  • Patience — Respecting time, process, and limits without tolerating avoidable harm.
  • Humility — Learning, admitting limits, and being corrected without humiliation.
  • Discipline — Repeated worthwhile action rather than punishment or performance.

Desire & difficult feelings

Craving, sobriety, anger, grief, and the possibilities of an honest response.

  • Craving — The pull of wanting, avoidance, and imagined completion.
  • Sobriety — Recovery as lived honesty, support, and protecting one's next choice.
  • Anger — Recognizing heat without feeding retaliation or denying legitimate hurt.
  • Grief — Loss and love without a requirement to find a lesson or a quick resolution.

Perspective & paradox

Uncertainty, contradiction, play, and solitude without easy resolution.

  • Uncertainty — Living and choosing without complete knowledge.
  • Paradox — Holding competing truths without flattening them into a slogan.
  • Play & freedom — Experiment, humor, and loosening rigid identities.
  • Solitude — Being alone, listening inwardly, and distinguishing solitude from isolation.

Meaning & the sacred

Gratitude, trust, integrity, and purpose while respecting distinct beliefs.

  • Gratitude — Recognizing what sustains life without denying suffering.
  • Trust & the sacred — Faith, mystery, and relationship with God where personally meaningful; not a doctrine shared by all authors.
  • Integrity — Alignment between conduct, commitments, and what one says matters.
  • Purpose & contribution — Participation in a larger life without demanding one final explanation.
