Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 60

Governing a great state is like cooking small fish. Let the kingdom be governed according to the Tao, and the manes of the departed will not manifest their spiritual energy. It is not that those manes have not that spiritual energy, but it will not be employed to hurt men. It is not that it could not hurt men, but neither does the ruling sage hurt them. When these two do not injuriously affect each other, their good influences converge in the virtue (of the Tao).

What it's pointing at

The chapter teaches that good governance—like cooking delicate fish—requires a light touch and trust in natural order rather than heavy-handed control. When a ruler aligns with the Tao instead of imposing force, even potential sources of harm (the departed, resentments, conflicts) lose their destructive power not because they disappear, but because they're met with non-injury. The deepest peace comes not from eliminating opposition but from refusing to amplify it.

Read against today

We live in a time of constant assertion—ideologies loudly competing, grievances amplified, every faction convinced its intensity is necessary. The chapter suggests our modern condition reflects a cooking that's too hot, a governance that stirs too much. We watch fear and anger spread not because the underlying tensions are new, but because we keep agitating them with counter-force. The world's current fractures may be less about the problems themselves and more about our collective insistence on forcing solutions rather than allowing the natural settling that comes from restraint and alignment with something larger than our own will.

To carry today

Today, notice where you might be stirring rather than allowing. In a conversation, in your own mind, with a difficulty—can you practice the cook's lightest touch, responding without the extra force born of fear? Trust that your presence itself, when calm and aligned, is already doing the work.

The Old Fisherman (excerpt)

Thereupon he followed the old man down the shore, catching him up just as he was drawing in his boat with his staff. Perceiving Confucius, the old man turned round to receive him, at which Confucius stepped back and prostrated himself twice before advancing.

"What do you want, Sir?" asked the fisherman.

"Just now, venerable Sir," replied Confucius, "you left without finishing your remarks. In my stupidity I cannot make out what you mean. Therefore I have come in the humble hope of hearing any words with which you may deign to help me."

"Well," said the old man, "you are certainly anxious to learn."

At this Confucius prostrated himself twice, and when he got up said, "Yes, I have been a student from my youth upwards until now, the sixty-ninth year of my age. Yet I have never heard the true doctrine, which I am now ready to receive without bias."

"Like species follow like," answered the old man. "Like sounds respond to like.

See p. 283, and the experiment of the two lutes, p. 319.

This is a law of nature. I will now with your leave apply what I know to what you occupy yourself with,—the affairs of men.

What it's pointing at

The old fisherman teaches Confucius that alignment with natural law is not achieved through study or effort, but through becoming the kind of person to whom truth naturally responds—like tuning a lute so that only the right note will resonate. He is pointing at a recognition: you cannot grasp the Tao by chasing it, only by becoming the vessel that it naturally flows into.

Read against today

In recovery and in contemplative life, we encounter this repeatedly: the moment we stop grasping for change and instead tend carefully to our own alignment, the shift we needed begins to arrive unbidden. The work is not to force transformation but to remove the static, the interference, the noise we generate through striving. When we become still enough, the resonance finds us.

The thread between them

Both texts teach non-interference as the deepest governance—the Tao chapter through the delicate image of cooking small fish (handle lightly, don't disturb), the fisherman through the image of the lute (be in tune, and resonance is automatic). The same law of nature operates in both: governance and wisdom succeed not through forceful application but through removing what prevents natural alignment.
