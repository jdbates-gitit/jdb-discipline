Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 77

May not the Way (or Tao) of Heaven be compared to the (method of) bending a bow? The (part of the bow) which was high is brought low, and what was low is raised up. (So Heaven) diminishes where there is superabundance, and supplements where there is deficiency. It is the Way of Heaven to diminish superabundance, and to supplement deficiency. It is not so with the way of man. He takes away from those who have not enough to add to his own superabundance. Who can take his own superabundance and therewith serve all under heaven? Only he who is in possession of the Tao! Therefore the (ruling) sage acts without claiming the results as his; he achieves his merit and does not rest (arrogantly) in it:--he does not wish to display his superiority.

What it's pointing at

Heaven naturally balances itself—it levels what's excessive and lifts what's lacking. Human nature tends to work the opposite way: we hoard and take from those who have little to add to our already-full stores. The chapter asks: who has the wisdom and character to reverse this? Only someone grounded in the Tao—someone who acts without ego, who doesn't cling to credit or need to prove themselves superior. That person can share abundance freely with the whole world.

Read against today

We live in a time of visible imbalance—wealth concentrates while need grows, voices amplify in echo chambers, those with platforms multiply their reach while the exhausted grow quieter. Our culture constantly teaches us that more is safety, that keeping ahead requires taking first. The Tao Te Ching holds up a mirror: this way of constant grasping, this refusal to let excess flow where it's needed, this compulsion to display superiority—these are the exact opposite of how nature heals itself. The chapter's quiet challenge cuts through all our arguments: can we imagine a different way of living together?

To carry today

Today, notice one place where you're holding on—a fear, a possession, a story about yourself you're defending. Just notice it without judgment. Then ask: what would it feel like to let that tightness go, even slightly, even briefly? The Tao works through release, not grip.

Heraclitus · no contact, same mountain

(82) It is a weariness to labour for the same masters and be ruled by them.

What it's pointing at

Heraclitus points to a specific exhaustion: the weariness of laboring under masters, of being ruled by them repeatedly. It names a condition of servitude not as moral failure but as a kind of wearing-down—the fatigue that comes from ceaseless obedience to external powers. The passage assumes that this weariness is real, that the structure itself (masters, rule, labor) generates a depletion that reason alone cannot overcome.

Read against today

Modern life often disguises its masters—systems, metrics, expectations—as neutral or self-chosen. The weariness Heraclitus names accumulates quietly: the fatigue of performing for approval, of optimizing for external measures, of serving invisible hierarchies while believing yourself free. This exhaustion shows up not as acute crisis but as a chronic dimming, a loss of aliveness that we mistake for maturity or realism.

The Echo · Chuang Tzu
Exercise of Faculties (excerpt)

Those of old who were called retired scholars, were not men who hid their bodies, or kept back their words, or concealed their wisdom. It was that the age was not suitable for their mission. If the age was suitable and their mission a success over the empire, they simply effaced themselves in the unity which prevailed. If the age was unsuitable and their mission at failure, they fell back upon their own resources and waited. Such is the way to preserve oneself.

Those of old who preserved themselves, did not...

Chuang Tzu's 'retired scholars' did not hide because they were superior; they 'fell back upon their own resources and waited' when the age was unsuitable for their work. This echoes Heraclitus's weariness: both name a recognition that certain structures cannot be served without exhaustion, and both propose that the response is not rebellion but a kind of strategic withdrawal—a refusal to expend oneself laboring under unsuitable rule.

The Confluence
Three voices, one morning — without making them say the same thing.

Where they meet

All three converge on a single diagnosis: there is a way things naturally balance (Heaven diminishes excess, supplements lack), but human arrangements perversely reverse this. Heraclitus's weariness, Chuang Tzu's withdrawal, and the Tao's diminishment all point to the same problem—a structure that demands constant expenditure of self in the wrong direction—and all propose that the answer is not struggle but a return to natural proportion, whether through non-action, waiting, or ceasing to labor for masters.

Where they part

The Tao Te Ching addresses the sage who can act and redistribute; it trusts that right action flows from alignment with the Way. Heraclitus names only the weariness itself—he does not promise a path out. Chuang Tzu proposes withdrawal and waiting, but his 'retired scholar' is still individual, still preserving a self. The Tao asks: who can take superabundance and serve all under heaven? The other two ask: how do I stop being diminished by serving what exhausts me?

To carry today

Today, notice where you are laboring for a master—internal or external—and feel the weariness without immediately naming it wrong or fixing it. Can you distinguish between the tiredness of genuine work and the specific depletion Heraclitus names? What would it look like to fall back on your own resources for a day, not to escape, but to feel what remains when you stop serving?
