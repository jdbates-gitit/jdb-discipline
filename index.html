Tao Te Ching · Legge translation
Chapter 64

That which is at rest is easily kept hold of; before a thing has given indications of its presence, it is easy to take measures against it; that which is brittle is easily broken; that which is very small is easily dispersed. Action should be taken before a thing has made its appearance; order should be secured before disorder has begun. The tree which fills the arms grew from the tiniest sprout; the tower of nine storeys rose from a (small) heap of earth; the journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step. He who acts (with an ulterior purpose) does harm; he who takes hold of a thing (in the same way) loses his hold. The sage does not act (so), and therefore does no harm; he does not lay hold (so), and therefore does not lose his hold. (But) people in their conduct of affairs are constantly ruining them when they are on the eve of success. If they were careful at the end, as (they should be) at the beginning, they would not so ruin them. Therefore the sage desires what (other men) do not desire, and does not prize things difficult to get; he learns what (other men) do not learn, and turns back to what the multitude of men have passed by. Thus he helps the natural development of all things, and does not dare to act (with an ulterior purpose of his own).

What it's pointing at

This chapter teaches that prevention is far easier than cure, and that forcing things causes them to slip away. The sage acts without grasping—moving with natural unfolding rather than against it. The wisdom here is paradoxical: by not trying to possess or control outcomes, we actually preserve what matters. The great ruin often comes at the moment of near-success, when vigilance gives way to carelessness or when hunger for the prize overrides the patience that got us there.

Read against today

Our age is one of constant reaction, of seizing at problems that have already exploded into crisis. We see this in how collective life fractures—the deeper tensions were visible long ago, but we waited to act until everything hardened into positions and resentment. The chapter's call to tend small things before they become large feels especially tender now, when so many grasp desperately at solutions, at winning arguments, at forcing outcomes, only to find these very grasping makes things worse. There is a cultural exhaustion visible in this—the constant striving without rest, the inability to see what is already breaking before the break becomes total. What if we learned to notice the tiny imbalances, the small unkindnesses, the early signs of disconnection, and tended those with gentleness instead?

To carry today

Today, notice one small thing that is already beginning to fray—in yourself, in a relationship, in something you care for. Rather than rushing to fix or control it, simply tend it with the care you would give a seedling. See if you can move toward it without grasping, with the kind of attention that asks nothing in return.

Chuang Tzu · same root, different voice
The Evidence of Virtue Complete (excerpt)

Hui Tzŭ said to Chuang Tzŭ, "Are there then men who have no passions?"

Chuang Tzŭ replied, "Certainly."

"But if a man has no passions," argued Hui Tzŭ, "what is it that makes him a man?"

"Tao," replied Chuang Tzŭ, "gives him his expression, and God gives him his form. How should he not be a man?"

"If then he is a man," said Hui Tzŭ, "how can he be without passions?"

"What you mean by passions," answered Chuang Tzŭ, "is not what I mean. By a man without passions I mean one who does not permit good and evil to disturb his internal economy, but rather falls in with whatever happens, as a matter of course, and does not add to the sum of his mortality."

The play of passion would tend to create conditions which otherwise would not exist.

"But whence is man to get his body," asked Hui Tzŭ, "if there is to be no adding to the sum of mortality?"

This is of course a gibe. Hui Tzŭ purposely takes Chuang Tzŭ's words à double entente.

"Tao gives him his expression," said Chuang Tzŭ, "and God gives him his form. He does not permit good and evil to disturb his internal economy. But now you are devoting your intelligence to externals, and wearing out your mental powers. You prop yourself against a tree and mutter, or lean over a table with half-closed eyes.

What it's pointing at

Chuang Tzu is describing a person whose internal life isn't constantly reorganized by the binary sorting of events into 'good' and 'evil.' Such a person isn't numb or removed—Tao still gives expression, God still gives form—but they don't add extra momentum to situations through the friction of their preferences and aversions. The passions Chuang Tzu warns against aren't feeling itself, but the habit of using feeling as a tool to remake the world according to an internal demand.

Read against today

In recovery, we learn that the urge to control outcomes—to lock down a result before it arrives, to keep the unwanted thing from even forming—is often the very mechanism that creates suffering. This passage suggests something subtler: that the exhaustion people feel isn't from life itself, but from the constant low-level campaign to make life conform. When we stop adding our agenda to what's already happening, something in us naturally settles.

The thread between them

Both texts warn against the same trap—the Tao chapter through the image of the person who 'ruins things on the eve of success' through unseeing grasping, the Chuang Tzu through the image of Hui Tzŭ 'propping himself against a tree and muttering' while wearing out his mental powers. The same teaching of non-striving arrives compressed as aphorism in one text and embodied as a portrait of exhaustion in the other.
